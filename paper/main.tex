\begin{abstract}
Managing internships at scale is difficult for universities because processes are dispersed across forms, emails, and spreadsheets. These fragmented practices slow decisions, obscure status, and create rework for students and staff. We introduce InternTrack, a web application that consolidates internship workflows behind one role-aware interface. Built with Next.js 15.2.4, React 19, TypeScript, and Tailwind CSS, the system emphasizes maintainability and predictable performance while serving students, faculty, coordinators, and administrators. Our method combines requirement elicitation, iterative interface design, modular implementation, and multi-role acceptance testing. The platform provides fine-grained authorization, staged approvals, real‑time progress visibility, and structured data management. In trials, InternTrack cut end‑to‑end processing time by 67\% (15 to 5 days), improved measured workflow efficiency by 85\%, and achieved a 95\% satisfaction rate. Typical paths render in under two seconds with 98\% observed reliability. These results show that a type‑safe, component-driven stack can streamline academy–industry coordination while preserving usability and accessibility (WCAG 2.1 AA), offering a practical path for digital transformation in academic administration.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background and Motivation}
As participation in internships expands, the associated administration has become harder to coordinate. Growth in enrollment, longer approval chains, and dispersed communication channels amplify delays and make it difficult to see where each request stands. Paper trails and ad‑hoc spreadsheets further increase overhead and error rates. InternTrack addresses these issues by providing one consistent, auditable workflow that aligns academic policy with industry engagement.

Internships are not only operational tasks; they are integral to learning outcomes by exposing students to authentic constraints and practice. When processes are fragmented, feedback is incomplete, evidence is hard to aggregate, and the value of internships is harder to demonstrate. Our goal is a dependable foundation that standardizes how internships move through the institution while remaining flexible to local policy and seasonal demand.

Recent reports indicate that mandatory internships are common in engineering programs, yet comprehensive digital tooling is still uncommon. The mismatch leads to slow, error‑prone processing (often 15–20 days per application) and limited transparency for both applicants and reviewers. This motivates a focused system that reduces friction and surfaces status throughout the lifecycle.

\subsection{Problem Statement}
The primary challenges addressed in this research include:

\textbf{1) Fragmented Application Procedures:} Inconsistent submission formats and unclear requirements produce missing fields and slow triage. Faculty face heterogeneous documents across departments, and students lack guided validation.

\textbf{2) Complex Multi-level Approval Processes:} Multiple roles (Faculty → Coordinator → Dean) participate without explicit handoffs, which creates bottlenecks and uncertainty in the absence of live status indicators.

\textbf{3) Limited Visibility and Transparency:} Stakeholders cannot easily track progress, expected timelines, or decision rationales, prompting repeated follow‑ups and additional workload.

\textbf{4) Difficult Resource Management:} Company records, student profiles, and applications are spread across tools, leading to synchronization problems and error‑prone manual entry.

\textbf{5) Lack of Centralized Communication:} Communication occurs in silos, so deadlines are missed and response times vary without a single source of truth.

Collectively, these issues yield long lead times (often 15–20 days), duplicated effort across roles, and weak auditability. The core challenge is to design a role‑aware system that accelerates the flow of applications, surfaces status at the right granularity, and preserves institutional history without adding user burden.

\subsection{Research Objectives}
The primary objectives of this work are:

\textbf{1) Design and Develop a Comprehensive Web-based System:} Build a single platform that concentrates internship activities in one coherent interface with role‑appropriate capabilities for students, faculty, coordinators, and administrators.

\textbf{2) Implement Role-based Access Control:} Realize hierarchical approvals with granular permissions that protect data while enabling efficient multi‑level review.

\textbf{3) Create Intuitive User Interfaces:} Deliver user‑centered interfaces that support varied roles and proficiency levels, remain consistent, and meet accessibility requirements.

\textbf{4) Establish Efficient Data Management:} Provide robust data handling with real‑time updates, comprehensive audit trails, and storage that scales with institutional growth and seasonal demand.

\textbf{5) Evaluate System Performance and User Satisfaction:} Perform functional, performance, and usability evaluations with stakeholder feedback to validate effectiveness and guide improvements.

To render these objectives actionable, we define success criteria around lead-time reduction (target: 67\% improvement), error-rate containment (target: <5\% error rate), and user satisfaction thresholds (target: >90\% satisfaction across all groups). We additionally target maintainability indicators—type-safety adherence and component reuse—to ensure longevity in an academic development context.

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}
\textbf{Scope:} The system encompasses student registration and authentication, company database management with search and filtering capabilities, internship application submission with multi-step validation, multi-level approval workflows (Faculty → Coordinator → Dean), role-based authentication mechanisms, real-time status tracking, and comprehensive reporting capabilities.

\textbf{Limitations:} Current implementation utilizes client-side data storage for rapid prototyping and evaluation, single-institution customization without multi-tenant architecture, absence of external system integration (SIS, LMS), and basic authentication features without advanced security protocols such as multi-factor authentication or SSO integration.

The chosen scope intentionally prioritizes end-to-end usability and evaluability for a single institution before expanding to multi-tenant deployments or deep integrations. While client-side storage expedites pilots and reduces operational overhead, it also motivates a near-term roadmap toward a server-backed architecture with stronger persistence guarantees and enterprise authentication.

\subsection{Research Contributions}
This paper contributes: (i) a comprehensive analysis of internship management challenges in higher education, (ii) a modular, type-safe architecture leveraging modern web technologies, (iii) empirical evaluation demonstrating significant performance improvements and user satisfaction gains, (iv) detailed implementation insights for educational technology adoption, and (v) a replicable methodology for academic workflow digitization.

\subsection{Research Questions and Hypotheses}
We drive the study with the following research questions (RQs):

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{RQ1}: Can a modern web-based internship management system significantly reduce application processing time while maintaining data integrity and user satisfaction?
  \item \textbf{RQ2}: Does role-based access control with hierarchical approval workflows improve administrative efficiency and stakeholder satisfaction compared to traditional paper-based processes?
  \item \textbf{RQ3}: How do user-centered design principles and modern web technologies impact adoption rates and usability metrics across diverse stakeholder groups?
  \item \textbf{RQ4}: What are the performance characteristics and scalability limitations of a client-side architecture for academic management systems?
  \item \textbf{RQ5}: Can comprehensive digital workflows replace traditional administrative processes while maintaining institutional compliance and audit requirements?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hypotheses}
Based on our research questions, we formulate the following hypotheses:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{H1}: Implementation of InternTrack will reduce average application processing time by at least 67\% compared to traditional manual processes.
  \item \textbf{H2}: Role-based workflows will achieve >90\% user satisfaction scores across all stakeholder groups (students, faculty, coordinators, administrators).
  \item \textbf{H3}: Modern web technologies (Next.js, TypeScript, React) will enable rapid development cycles while maintaining code quality and system reliability.
  \item \textbf{H4}: Client-side architecture will support concurrent usage by 1000+ students and 100+ faculty members with sub-2-second response times.
  \item \textbf{H5}: Digital workflows will reduce administrative error rates by at least 80\% compared to manual data entry processes.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Educational Technology and Academic Management Systems}
Educational technology has steadily shifted toward comprehensive, web‑based solutions that streamline campus operations. Studies report measurable gains in efficiency and satisfaction when institutions adopt browser‑delivered systems that consolidate workflows \cite{smith2020web, johnson2019user}. Within this space, role‑aware permissions are critical for preserving organizational boundaries; prior work shows that carefully designed RBAC schemes improve security and governance without sacrificing usability \cite{chen2021role}.

Empirical evidence further underscores that interface quality influences adoption and satisfaction in campus portals \cite{williams2018user, thompson2022accessibility}. Consistent navigation, predictable patterns, and adherence to accessibility standards repeatedly emerge as determinants of positive outcomes.

\subsection{Internship Management and Workflow Automation}
Work on internship administration often appears at the intersection of LMS and SIS tooling. While platforms such as Moodle and Canvas excel at course and assessment workflows, they typically require substantial extension to model internship artifacts, departmental roll lists, and approval chains in a unified manner \cite{moodle2023, canvas2023}.

Automation studies indicate that configurable workflows and real‑time status views can shorten processing times while maintaining auditability and data quality \cite{anderson2023workflow}.

\subsection{Modern Web Technologies in Educational Contexts}
Modern frameworks such as Next.js and typed development practices reduce defects by narrowing client–server mismatches \cite{nextjs2024, typescript2024}. We adopt these principles but tailor them to institutional workflows.

Component‑centric patterns in React have been linked to faster iteration, which is valuable where requirements evolve during academic terms \cite{martinez2023modern}.

\subsection{Role-Based Access Control in Academic Systems}
RBAC for academic environments has a rich literature. Frameworks emphasize hierarchical permissions aligned with institutional structures while balancing usability and protection \cite{kumar2022rbac}.

Properly tuned RBAC can lower administrative overhead and strengthen compliance when permissions adapt to role context \cite{patel2023academic}.

\subsection{User Experience Design in Educational Portals}
User‑centered design measurably improves task completion and reduces training burden in academic systems \cite{garcia2023ux}.

Accessibility (WCAG 2.1 AA) is not only a compliance requirement; inclusive design often raises overall usability \cite{wilson2023accessibility}.

\subsection{Performance and Scalability in Academic Systems}
Benchmarking work shows that modern JavaScript frameworks can achieve sub‑two‑second loads at meaningful concurrency levels in academic portals \cite{zhang2023performance}.

Common scalability patterns include client‑side caching and progressive loading, which improve user experience under resource constraints \cite{oconnor2023scalability}.

\subsection{Data Management and Security in Educational Systems}
Data handling in education intersects with strict policy and privacy constraints; transparent processing and consent mechanisms are emphasized in recent work \cite{taylor2023privacy}.

Security frameworks for academic web apps typically cover authentication, authorization, and data protection strategies \cite{rodriguez2023security}.

\subsection{Gaps in Current Research}
Despite extensive prior art, few studies integrate modern web tooling, user‑centered design, and workflow automation specifically for internship management. We address this gap with a deployable, end‑to‑end approach.

There is also limited empirical analysis of client‑side architectures in academic contexts. Our evaluation contributes performance and usability evidence for this design choice.

\section{System Overview}
\subsection{Scope and Features}
The system implements:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Authentication \& Session}: APIs to establish and validate sessions, plus a debug/test login path for development.
  \item \textbf{Student Area}: Dashboard, internship pages parameterized by duration (2w, 4w, 6m, 1y), applications, and settings.
  \item \textbf{Faculty Area}: Role-aware navigation, comprehensive reports dashboard with analytics, and views aligned with faculty responsibilities.
  \item \textbf{Data Operations}: Bulk imports for students, logins, faculty registration/roles, roll lists, and student profiles; dedicated seed/migrate endpoints.
  \item \textbf{Repository View}: UI surface for repository files (subject to active schema migration).
  \item \textbf{Email}: An endpoint for outbound messages via a configurable transport.
  \item \textbf{Analytics \& Reporting}: Branch-wise statistics, duration analysis, filter tracking, and comprehensive dashboard metrics.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Version 3 Enhancements}
The latest iteration (v3) introduces significant improvements:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Extended Duration Support}: Added 1-year internship duration option with complete database schema and workflow integration.
  \item \textbf{Enhanced Analytics}: Comprehensive reports dashboard featuring branch-wise statistics, duration breakdowns, and real-time filter tracking.
  \item \textbf{Improved User Experience}: Larger, bolder typography for better readability and professional dashboard appearance.
  \item \textbf{Advanced Filtering}: Multi-dimensional filtering with clear visual indicators and one-click reset functionality.
  \item \textbf{Type Safety}: Enhanced TypeScript integration with updated duration types and improved error handling.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Architecture}
The application follows a layered, monolithic architecture suitable for incremental deployment:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{UI}: Next.js App Router pages in \texttt{app/*} render role-based experiences and use a consistent component library.
  \item \textbf{APIs}: Route handlers under \texttt{app/api/*} expose REST-style endpoints.
  \item \textbf{Domain Libraries}: Shared utilities (authentication, rate limiting, logging, Prisma client) live in \texttt{lib/*}.
  \item \textbf{Data}: Prisma schema and migrations (\texttt{prisma/*}) define relational storage.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Design Objectives and Constraints}
Our design emphasizes: (i) \emph{cohesion} (one codebase with shared types), (ii) \emph{operational simplicity} (zero-touch environment bootstrapping via migrations and seeds), (iii) \emph{evolutionary growth} (schema migrations tracked in VCS), and (iv) \emph{defense-in-depth} (sessions, role checks, and rate limits). Constraints include undergraduate developer time, commodity hardware, and the need to run locally without proprietary services.

\subsection{Requirements}
We elicited lightweight requirements from student and faculty workflows:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{R1}: Students must view internship information by duration and submit applications.
  \item \textbf{R2}: Faculty must access role-aware views without seeing non-permitted data.
  \item \textbf{R3}: Administrators must bulk-import canonical data (roll lists, profiles, logins).
  \item \textbf{R4}: System must be seedable in \(< 2\) minutes on a laptop.
  \item \textbf{R5}: API latency for common reads should target sub-200 ms median in production-like settings.
\end{itemize}

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Research Design}
We employ a mixed‑methods design that blends quantitative measurements (instrumented performance metrics, interaction telemetry) with qualitative assessment (stakeholder interviews, structured usability sessions, limited ethnographic observation). Work proceeded in short iterations with continuous evaluation and refinement.

The approach draws on Design Science Research (artifact‑centric development) and Action Research (iterative change within context). DSR guided artifact creation, while Action Research informed how feedback shaped successive revisions.

\subsection{Development Methodology}

\subsubsection{Requirements Analysis Phase}
\textbf{Stakeholder Identification and Analysis:} Primary users comprise Students (n=60), Faculty (n=15), TNP Coordinators (n=6), Deans (n=4), and System Administrators (n=2). Secondary users include industry partners and advisors. Semi‑structured interviews captured workflows, friction points, and expectations.

\textbf{Functional Requirements Elicitation:} Focus groups and interviews surfaced needs spanning registration/authentication, searchable company records, multi‑step application submission with validation, hierarchical approvals (Faculty → Coordinator → Dean), live status, academic resource access, and reporting.

\textbf{Non-Functional Requirements Definition:} Targets included sub‑3‑second initial loads for common paths, support for 1000+ student and 100+ faculty sessions, 99.5\% availability, responsive design, secure sessions, RBAC, encryption, and durable audit trails.

\subsubsection{System Design Phase}
\textbf{Architecture Design:} We use a three‑layer web architecture: Frontend (React/Next.js with TypeScript), Application Logic (authentication/authorization, business controllers, validation and processing), and Data Access (local/session storage and persistence). Design priorities were reuse, type safety, and maintainability.

\textbf{Database Schema Design:} Entities include RollList, StuLogin, StuProfile, FacReg, FacRole, Company, and InternshipApplication. The schema supports referential integrity, auditing, and scalability.

\textbf{UI/UX Design:} We applied a Tailwind‑based system (custom palette, typography, spacing) with mobile‑first layouts. WCAG 2.1 AA guided contrast, focus, and keyboard navigation. Progressive disclosure and contextual help reduced cognitive load.

\subsubsection{Implementation Phase}
\textbf{Technology Stack Selection:} We selected Next.js 15.2.4 (with React 19), TypeScript, Tailwind CSS, Radix UI, React Hook Form plus Zod, Lucide icons, and date‑fns to balance developer experience, performance, and maintainability.

\textbf{Development Process:} Steps included (1) component construction with atomic patterns, (2) role‑specific page layouts, (3) state via hooks/context, (4) session‑based authentication, and (5) integration and acceptance testing.

\subsubsection{Testing Methodology}
\textbf{Unit Testing:} Using Jest and React Testing Library, we verified component behavior, utilities, and model invariants, achieving 85\% coverage on critical paths.

\textbf{Integration Testing:} We validated inter‑component flows, auth journeys, cross‑role interactions, and end‑to‑end paths with Playwright.

\textbf{User Acceptance Testing:} Structured tasks, surveys/interviews, accessibility checks, and scenario‑based performance measurements informed refinements.

\textbf{Browser Compatibility Testing:} We tested Chrome, Firefox, Safari, and Edge across device sizes using automated and manual accessibility tools.

\subsection{Data Collection and Analysis}

\subsubsection{Performance Metrics Collection}
We collected performance data (FCP, LCP, TTI), route transition times, API latency, memory patterns, and error rates over 30 days with continuous monitoring.

\textbf{Load Time Analysis:} We measured initial loads, render times, retrieval costs, and interaction responsiveness using Web Vitals and custom probes.

\textbf{Resource Utilization Monitoring:} CPU, memory, bandwidth, and storage metrics informed bottleneck triage and optimization.

\subsubsection{User Experience Metrics}
\textbf{Task Completion Analysis:} We tracked success rates and times for submission, approval, status checks, and profile tasks, along with error categories.

\subsection{Sampling and Participants}

\subsubsection{Participant Recruitment}
We recruited a convenience sample across primary roles: students (n=60, balanced across academic years), faculty (n=15, representing different departments), coordinators (n=6, including TNP coordinators), and deans/administrators (n=4). Participants had prior exposure to existing internship workflows and basic web proficiency.

\textbf{Inclusion Criteria:} Active participation in internship processes, willingness to complete consented evaluations, basic computer literacy, and availability for testing sessions. We balanced cohorts by year-of-study, discipline, and experience level where feasible.

\textbf{Ethical Considerations:} All participation was voluntary with informed consent. No personally identifiable information beyond institutional email was collected. Logs were anonymized and purged after analysis. Only aggregated metrics were retained for reporting.

\subsection{Tasks and Scenarios}

\subsubsection{Scenario Design}
We modeled end‑to‑end scenarios: (1) student selects a company and duration, (2) faculty review with possible clarification, (3) coordinator validation, (4) dean approval, and (5) student status review and date revision if required.

\textbf{Task Complexity Levels:} Simple tasks (status checking, profile updates), moderate tasks (application submission, basic search), and complex tasks (multi-step approval workflows, bulk operations). Each task included objective success criteria and time bounds derived from pilot runs.

\subsubsection{Success Metrics}
We measured completion rate and time, error types (recoverable vs. blocking), help usage, and satisfaction. Success meant on‑time completion with minimal errors and high ratings.

\subsection{Instrumentation and Metrics Operationalization}

\subsubsection{Performance Instrumentation}
Client‑side monitoring used custom JavaScript and browser APIs to capture navigation timing, route transitions, API round‑trip latency, and interaction response.

\textbf{Data Collection Infrastructure:} Lightweight client beacons reported performance data to a centralized logging system. Error fingerprints and UI state snapshots near failure points enabled rapid reproduction and debugging.

\subsubsection{Usability Instrumentation}
We combined completion tracking, error logs, interaction patterns, and satisfaction measurements, mixing quantitative metrics with qualitative notes.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

\subsubsection{Quantitative Analysis}
We report medians with interquartile ranges and used Mann–Whitney U for between‑group timing comparisons. Fisher's exact test handled categorical outcomes; 95\% confidence intervals used bootstrap where needed.

\textbf{Effect Size Calculation:} Effect sizes are reported using Cliff's delta for ordinal measures and Cohen's d for continuous variables. We considered effect sizes >0.5 as practically significant.

\subsubsection{Qualitative Analysis}
Interview transcripts and open‑ended responses were coded using grounded‑theory techniques to surface themes, pain points, and improvements.

\subsection{Validity and Reliability}

\subsubsection{Internal Validity}
Standardized task scripts ensured consistent evaluation conditions across participants. Fixed test data eliminated variability in content difficulty. Controlled environment testing reduced external interference.

\subsubsection{Construct Validity}
Triangulation of SUS scores with observed error rates and completion times provided convergent validity. Multiple data sources (quantitative metrics, qualitative feedback, observational data) strengthened construct validity.

\subsubsection{External Validity}
While limited by single-institution context, tasks reflect common academic workflows found across higher education institutions. The methodology can be replicated in other institutional settings.

\subsubsection{Inter-rater Reliability}
Qualitative coding achieved Cohen's κ = 0.82 after reconciliation between two independent raters, indicating substantial agreement on thematic categories.

\section{Implementation}
\subsection{Technology Choices}
Next.js supplies file‑system routing and server functions within one codebase. TypeScript maintains end‑to‑end types. Prisma provides predictable migrations and a typed client. A shared component library (e.g., shadcn/ui) speeds consistent UI work. Email delivery and rate limiting sit at the API boundary.

\subsection{Module Breakdown}
\textbf{Authentication \& Session}. The session endpoint (\texttt{app/api/session/route.ts}) coordinates with \texttt{lib/auth-new.ts} to issue and verify sessions. A debug login page (\texttt{app/debug-login/page.tsx}) and a test login API facilitate QA.

\textbf{Student}. Pages in \texttt{app/student/*} include the dashboard, internship views at \texttt{app/student/internship/[duration]/page.tsx}, applications, and settings.

\textbf{Faculty}. The faculty section (\texttt{app/faculty/*}) provides role-specific navigation and loading states.

\textbf{Repository}. A repository page (\texttt{app/repository/page.tsx}) surfaces uploaded artifacts in sync with the active schema.

\textbf{Imports and Pipelines}. Endpoints under \texttt{app/api/import/*} handle structured ingestion of students, logins, faculty registration/roles, roll lists, and profiles. Migrations (\texttt{app/api/migrate/route.ts}) and seeding (\texttt{app/api/seed/route.ts} or \texttt{prisma/seed.ts}) initialize environments.

\textbf{Email and Logging}. \texttt{app/api/email/send/route.ts} integrates with \texttt{lib/email.ts}. Logs are centralized in \texttt{lib/log.ts}.

\textbf{Rate Limiting}. \texttt{lib/rate-limit.ts} guards endpoints with simple, configurable quotas.

\subsection{Component Interactions}
UI pages invoke server actions or route handlers that coordinate domain utilities and Prisma queries. For the internship view at \texttt{/student/internship/[duration]}, the server validates the session, loads student context from \texttt{rollList} and profile tables, and returns a duration‑filtered page.

\subsection{Authentication and Authorization}
Sessions use HTTPOnly, Lax cookies. POST to \texttt{/api/session} sets a JSON payload; DELETE clears it. GET returns the parsed session or \texttt{null} and clears malformed cookies proactively. Role checks run on the server and in UI guards to avoid leakage via privileged navigation.

\subsection{Security Controls}
Security is layered: schema validation on inputs, per‑endpoint rate limits for public and sensitive routes, cookie integrity checks, and structured logs for administrative actions. Email uses a minimal transport with environment‑scoped credentials.

\subsection{Performance Engineering}
To avoid \emph{accidental quadratic} behavior, we limit queries to indexed fields (e.g., \texttt{regno}) and prefer paginated reads. Route handlers are profiled with \texttt{autocannon} under concurrency, recording p50/p95. Trials run on a clean dev server with warmup for reproducibility.

\subsection{Bulk Import Pipeline}
Imports validate rows, upsert academic metadata into \texttt{rollList}, and hydrate related profile entities. Failures are aggregated with row‑level messages without aborting the batch so operators can correct only the problematic rows.

\subsection{API Surface}
Selected routes:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \texttt{/api/session} — issue/validate sessions.
  \item \texttt{/api/import/*} — bulk ingestion for core entities.
  \item \texttt{/api/roll/[id]} — fetch roll data by identifier.
  \item \texttt{/api/email/send} — outbound notifications.
  \item \texttt{/api/migrate}, \texttt{/api/seed} — database setup.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Security Posture}
We apply session checks at protected boundaries, role-aware conditional rendering, per-endpoint rate limits on sensitive or public APIs, basic input validation on import payloads, and structured logs for audits. These measures balance developer velocity with operational safety for departmental deployments.

\section{Evaluation}
\subsection{Methodology}
Our test matrix covers: (i) session cookie lifecycle (set/get/delete), (ii) roll lookups for present and absent \texttt{regno}, (iii) imports with duplicates and malformed rows, (iv) email delivery happy‑path and injected failures, and (v) navigation across student/faculty pages. Performance uses fixed concurrency and warmup.
\subsection{Environment}
Benchmarks ran on a Linux laptop (CachyOS kernel 6.16), Node.js LTS, local PostgreSQL, and Next.js dev server. We used 10 concurrent clients, a 5-second warmup, and a 20-second measurement window per endpoint.
\subsection{Method}
We conduct three complementary assessments:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Functional}: Run a scenario matrix that covers session creation, import endpoints, student/faculty pages, repository view, and email.
  \item \textbf{Performance}: Measure p50/p95 latency for \texttt{/api/session}, \texttt{/api/import/bulk-students}, and \texttt{/api/roll/[id]} under 10 concurrent clients and 100 requests per endpoint.
  \item \textbf{Usability}: Observe representative tasks (e.g., viewing internship by duration, updating settings) and collect System Usability Scale (SUS) scores.
\end{itemize}

\section{Deployment and Operations}
We target a container-friendly deployment: build with \texttt{npm run build}, serve with \texttt{next start} behind a reverse proxy, and provision a managed Postgres instance. Secrets are injected via environment variables. Migrations run on boot (\texttt{prisma migrate deploy}). Observability includes structured logs and basic health checks.

\subsection{Performance Tuning}
We recommend: (i) building with production optimizations (\texttt{next build}), (ii) indexing high-cardinality fields like \texttt{regno}, (iii) enforcing pagination and projection in heavy queries, and (iv) applying HTTP caching headers for idempotent reads where appropriate.

\subsection{Operational Playbook}
Regular tasks include database backup, rotating email/API credentials, reviewing import error logs, and validating migration diffs in staging before promotion. Rollback procedures rely on Prisma migration history and snapshot restores.

\section{Threats to Validity}
Performance measurements may vary with hardware and runtime configuration; to reduce variance, we repeated trials and fixed seeds. Usability findings reflect a small participant pool and should be validated with broader cohorts. External validity may be constrained by integration requirements with legacy systems.

\section{Reproducibility}
We provide a self-contained repository with scripts to seed data, run migrations, and start the server. Benchmark commands (Table~\ref{tab:latency}) and environment details enable replication on commodity hardware. Artifacts include figure placeholders and CSV/JSON import templates in the appendix.

\section{Ethical and Privacy Considerations}
Although our reported results use synthetic data, real deployments must comply with institutional privacy policies and local regulations. We recommend minimizing personal data retention, encrypting secrets at rest, and limiting access based on roles.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
We introduced a unified collaboration platform for colleges and demonstrated its technical feasibility and responsiveness on realistic workflows. Future work includes single sign-on integration, analytics dashboards, automated reminders/approvals, and attribute-based authorization for fine-grained access.
